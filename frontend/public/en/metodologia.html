The network that went dark

Sources and Methodology

The code and data are in the project repository. The processing lives in datasets_y_procesamiento_de_datos, in notebooks that are run in order, and the app in frontend. The notebooks are written in Spanish.

  • 0_generacion_datasets.ipynb: downloads the sources and describes each dataset and its columns.
  • 1_datos_centrales.ipynb: builds the track, dates it and classifies the people of each census.
  • 1bis_otras_formas_de_fechar.ipynb: compares the nearest station with other ways of dating the track.
  • 2_analisis.ipynb: analyzes the results and validates them against the ONDaT and real maps.
  • 3_exportacion_frontend.ipynb: writes the data the app reads.

Transport SDI (IDE de Transporte)

Argentina’s national Transport Secretariat publishes two GeoJSON layers in its Spatial Data Infrastructure (IDE). The railway layer has track sections with their route, their current status (active or not operational) and their stated length in km. The stations layer has the stations that were built, with their name and position.

OpenStreetMap

Two OpenStreetMap queries, through the Overpass API. The first returns unused track sections in Argentina, with their route and status (disused, abandoned, razed or dismantled). The second returns train routes, with the tracks each one runs on and the type of service (passenger or not).

Download

Each link runs its query and may take several minutes. The app’s data was downloaded in October 2026.

  • Unused track
[out:json][timeout:10000];
area["ISO3166-1"="AR"][admin_level=2]->.a;
(
  way["railway"="abandoned"](area.a);
  way["railway"="disused"](area.a);
  way["railway"="razed"](area.a);
  way["railway"="dismantled"](area.a);
);
out geom;
  • Train routes
[out:json][timeout:10000];
relation["route"="train"](-55.1,-73.6,-21.7,-53.6);
out geom;

If a link fails, the query can be run by hand on overpass-turbo.eu (export as raw OSM data).

Wikidata

Wikidata, the Wikimedia Foundation’s open database, has Argentina’s train stations. For each one it gives the name and (when available) the geographic position, the opening or construction date, and the closing date.

Download

Run this query on query.wikidata.org. The app’s data was downloaded in October 2026.

SELECT ?st ?stLabel ?coord ?opened ?closed ?inception WHERE {
  ?st wdt:P31/wdt:P279* wd:Q55488 .   # is a train station (or subclass)
  ?st wdt:P17 wd:Q414 .               # country = Argentina
  OPTIONAL { ?st wdt:P625  ?coord }     # coordinates
  OPTIONAL { ?st wdt:P1619 ?opened }    # opened to the public
  OPTIONAL { ?st wdt:P3999 ?closed }    # official closure
  OPTIONAL { ?st wdt:P571  ?inception } # construction
  SERVICE wikibase:label { bd:serviceParam wikibase:language "es,en". }
}

The National Transport Data Observatory (Observatorio Nacional de Datos de Transporte, part of UTN) publishes a spreadsheet with the annual series of km of railway network in service, up to 2000. Motivation

The idea was to see how Argentina’s railway network evolved throughout its history (when it grew, when it went dark and who it left without a train). What you can find are maps and scattered data for some years, but nothing complete from the start of the network to the present. So we had to generate that data from what is published, and then validate the generated data.

How the data was generated

No source has the network for every year, but each one contributes a different piece. The track comes from two maps: the government’s has the track that exists today and OpenStreetMap adds the track that was torn up. The dates come from Wikidata’s stations: each section of track takes the opening and closing year of the nearest station that has them. With those two dates and the current status of each section (passenger, freight, inactive or torn up), the network of any year from 1857 to 2025 can be rebuilt.

For the people, each INDEC census tract is classified according to the stations within 10 km: with passenger trains, freight only, lost the train or never had a train. This is done with the network of each census year, from 1991 to 2022.

How it was validated

Against the official series

The ONDaT publishes the km of network in service for each year up to 2000. That series was not used for dating (to avoid overfitting) and served mostly as a check.

Against real maps

The generated present-day network was compared with the most recent maps that could be found. The track in service follows the same corridors as a 2026 map of operating corridors (source). Against a 2021 map (source) that uses the same four categories, the difference is almost entirely in passenger track, since several services have been suspended since then; 2_analisis.ipynb lists each one with the news report that confirms it.

Why the nearest station

Machine learning models that learn the year from the position were also tried. Beyond their error, they smooth the curve a lot, and the sudden closures of 1961, 1977 and 1993 are lost. The nearest station is the simplest to explain and the one that best follows the official curve.

Two examples, on a grid covering the whole country: the opening year, the closing year and the difference between the two that each place would get, along with its curve against the ONDaT.

Final notes

  • The result is an open, georeferenced, year-by-year reconstruction of Argentina’s railway network from 1857 to 2025, with the status of each section and cross-referenced with the people of four censuses. Until now, that history was told with maps or scattered data; with continuous data you can watch the network grow and go dark across the territory.
  • The track dates are estimates, and closing dates are coarser than opening dates because Wikidata has fewer of them. There is no date for when track was torn up, all track starts out with passenger service, and each section has only one change.
  • Other results that were not used in the app:

    Where each category lives

    Ranking people by the density of the place where they live, those who never had a train cluster in the most rural tracts, while those who lost the train or only have freight live in the towns and small cities in between.

    Other ways of showing the people

    Alternative ways of showing the people on the map were explored, such as equal 50 km hexagons, which don’t depend on department boundaries.

    Holding population fixed to see how it changes with historical track

    If you hold the people of one census fixed and lay the network of each year over them, you can see how much of the change comes from the tracks rather than from where people live.

From the analysis to the app

All the analysis was done in Python, in notebooks that are run in order: 0_generacion_datasets.ipynb downloads the sources, 1_datos_centrales.ipynb processes them, 2_analisis.ipynb analyzes and validates, and 3_exportacion_frontend.ipynb writes the data for the app.
